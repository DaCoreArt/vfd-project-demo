Introduction: Overview of the NYC Volunteer Fire Department Project  
In this course, we will examine the volunteer fire departments that serve mainly New York City’s coastal 
and geographically isolated communities. These volunteer fire departments must balance public safety 
responsibilities with financial sustainability. All of them are nonprofit organizations that serve as 
instructive experiential learning environments. These departments face accounting and financial 
management issues, such as replacing major emergency equipment, which can cost hundreds of thousands 
of dollars and is typically funded through grants, fundraising, and financing. 
In this project, students will build a six-year financial dataset covering all NYC volunteer firehouse 
nonprofits and analyze their financial sustainability using Excel. Students will also visit a local firehouse 
to understand its operations and equipment needs, which will inform their analysis. Students will simulate 
funding scenarios and assess the risk and financial readiness of these organizations to make significant 
equipment and other purchases. 
History of New York City Volunteer Fire Departments 
Per The Volunteer Firemen’s Association of the City of New York (2025) (VFACNY), volunteer fire 
departments began to play an increasingly important role in the fire service in New York City in the late 
1800s. At that time, the city’s volunteer fire departments were facing a number of challenges, including 
inadequate funding, poor equipment, and a lack of training opportunities. In response, a group of 
volunteer firefighters formed the VFACNY in order to advocate for the needs of volunteer fire 
departments and to provide support and resources to its members. 
The VFACNY was established to advocate for the rights and interests of volunteer firefighters, and to 
provide them with training, equipment and other resources. The organization’s early leaders worked to 
improve the conditions and status of volunteer firefighters in New York City.  
Over the years, the VFACNY has played a key role in advocating for better working conditions, and 
benefits for volunteer firefighters. It has also worked to improve the quality and availability of training for 
volunteer firefighters and to increase funding for volunteer fire departments. 
In addition to advocating for the interests of volunteer fire departments, the VFACNY has also played an 
important role in promoting the history and tradition of the fire service in New York City. The 
organization has organized events and ceremonies to commemorate the contributions of volunteer 
firefighters, and has worked to preserve the history and heritage of the fire service in the city. Throughout 
its history, the VFACNY has played an important role in supporting volunteer firefighters in New York 
City, and it continues to be a vital organization for the thousands of men and women who volunteer their 
time and expertise to protect their communities from the threat of fire. They work alongside career 
firefighters and provide vital services to residents and visitors. 
Despite these changes, the VFACNY and it’s departments play an important part of the fire service in 
New York City. Today, there are 8 volunteer fire departments across the city that work alongside the New 
York City Fire Department (FDNY), which is one of the largest fire departments in the world, staffed by 
over 11,000 career firefighters. The eight fire departments are: Broad Channel VFD & Ambulance Corp., 
Edgewater Park Volunteer Hose Company No. 1, Gerrittsen Beach Volunteer Fire Department, Point

NYC Volunteer Fire Departments Offer an Experience to Connect Theory to Practice 
 
Many accounting and finance students report difficulty connecting abstract financial theories to 
real-world decision-making, particularly in organizations that do not operate for profit. Traditional 
coursework often relies on short case studies that offer limited insight into how organizations function in 
practice. Compared to other fields (e.g., education, healthcare, and the physical sciences), accounting and 
finance programs offer relatively few opportunities for immersive, community-based experiences in both 
undergraduate and graduate settings (Foy et al., 2024; Foy et al., 2025). This gap is especially evident 
when students attempt to understand how financial management operates within mission-driven 
organizations such as volunteer fire departments. 
This project addresses that gap by placing students directly in the context of New York City 
volunteer fire departments, where financial decisions are closely tied to public safety, community trust, 
and resource constraints. Unlike traditional business environments, volunteer fire departments rely on a

Through this immersive, semester-long venue-based experiential learning project, students engage with a 
real organizations, observing how financial decisions are made, how funding priorities are established, 
and how operational needs (e.g., equipment, training, facilities) are balanced against financial realities. 
Rather than analyzing a static case, students develop an evolving understanding of how accounting 
and financial management issues function in a high-stakes, community-centered environment. We explore 
questions such as: How does a fire department fund a new apparatus? What trade-offs are made between 
equipment upgrades and community outreach? How do financial constraints influence emergency 
readiness? This sustained engagement allows students to connect theoretical concepts, such as budgeting, 
capital financing, and cost analysis, to real-world implications that directly impact public safety. 
By the end of the project, students are expected to develop a deeper, more practical understanding 
of how financial principles apply in nonprofit and public service contexts. In addition, the experience 
supports the development of transferable, employer-valued skills such as communication, critical 
thinking, adaptability, and professional judgment, particularly in environments where financial decisions 
carry significant social consequences.

Project Implementation 
 
Throughout the semester, students will study accounting and financial management concepts 
through assigned readings and course materials. This project complements those materials by providing a 
structured opportunity to apply theory in a real business context. 
The project consists of five deliverables spaced across the semester. Students will collect nonprofit 
tax return data on all NYC volunteer fire departments and analyze them, identify and visit a local fire 
department to familiarize themselves with fire department apparatus, analyze risk and funding scenarios, 
prepare a presentation on findings, and reflect on how the experience developed employer-desired 
competencies. 
While all deliverables are graded individually, collaboration by sharing experiences with fellow 
classmates is highly encouraged. This structure reflects real workplace environments, where individuals 
are evaluated on both independent performance and share experiences with others. 
A process flowchart on the following page summarizes the deliverable sequence, submission 
requirements, and grading criteria. The remainder of this document provides detailed instructions, 
templates, and grading rubrics. Reviewing the templates early is strongly recommended.

Purpose of the NYC Volunteer Fire Department Project 
 
The purpose of the NYC Volunteer Fire Department Project is to connect the abstract theories a 
student learns in a typical issues in nonprofit accounting and/or financial management course, 
use a venue-based (a place outside of the classroom) local fire house as a concrete experience, 
engage in active experimentation (applying theory), and reflect on the experiences. The project 
requires that students identify and visit an independent local firehouse and attempt to engage a 
firefighter to conduct a short tour of a firehouse and its operations. Inform the firefighter that you 
are studying nonprofit accounting and financial management in school, and you have been tasked 
to study the financial aspects of a small nonprofit organization, which requires a basic 
understanding of how a firehouse operates. The aim is for students to gain a more comprehensive 
understanding of accounting and finance issues related to nonprofit organizations, and observing 
and understanding a local volunteer firehouse serves as a great example. Although many 
government-run firehouses are not nonprofit organizations, the equipment and apparatus they use 
are similar and can be easily substituted for a volunteer firehouse.  
 
Another purpose of this project is to help all students further develop employer-desired 
competencies and be able to tell a visual and oral story to potential employers about how they 
learned nonprofit accounting and financial management theories and examples of their 
application.  
 
Project Learning Objectives 
 
1. Integrate employer-desired competencies to develop highly valued employer-desired skills. 
2. Construct and analyze a comprehensive financial profile of a nonprofit organization using 
real-world data and field-based observations. 
3. Analyze real nonprofit financial data and interpret nonprofit operations from a nonprofit 
accounting and financial management perspective. 
4. Defend evidence-based financial recommendations under realistic operational constraints in a 
nonprofit context. 
5. Communicate financial insights and reflect on experiential learning to demonstrate 
professional judgment, analytical thinking, and applied competency development.

Task D2.2: Firehouse Visitation 
 
In experiential learning, this is considered a venue-based, concrete task. Students conduct a field 
visit to a local Firehouse. A concrete experience is what you directly observe when you visit the 
firehouse, such as the fire truck apparatus, protective gear, and communications equipment. The 
concrete experience is designed to help you understand abstract concepts in your assigned 
readings.  
The purpose of the visit is to engage your external sensory perceptions, such as seeing, hearing, 
smelling, tasting (if applicable), and experiencing physical sensations such as touch, as well as 
your internal body’s natural awareness of movement, position, effort, balance, fatigue, emotions, 
all known as kinesthetic proprioception, as you observe a local firehouse in action.  
 
Why is engaging in your senses important for learning?  
 
Learning is deeper and more meaningful when it involves the whole body, not just the eyes and 
the mind. By paying attention to how work feels, such as the pace of movement, the physical 
effort required, the tightness of space, and the rhythm of tasks, these senses help you gain 
insights that cannot be captured by numbers alone. This bodily awareness helps you understand 
why accounting and finance outcomes appear as they do. Labor costs, productivity, errors, waste, 
pricing, and staffing decisions are all influenced by the physical realities of work. When you 
notice strain, repetition, smooth coordination, or congestion, you are observing the real forces 
that shape costs and revenues. 
 
Engaging your senses and bodily awareness during the visit is intended to set the stage for you to 
connect abstract financial concepts to real operational experience, thereby helping you transform 
accounting and financial management from theoretical to practical applications and to 
understand how a nonprofit business actually functions and generates revenue and incurs 
expenses. 
 
For those with an accommodation letter who cannot physically visit a local firehouse, there is a 
virtual option to complete this task. Virtual option: Students with accessibility limitations should 
rely on Google Maps, Yelp, a local firehouse’s website, or other means such as online reviews, 
images, and interview people who may have a close connection to the local firehouse chosen. 
You can also work with classmates who may be able to FaceTime their visitation live so you can 
share the experience. This option has been done successfully in prior coursework as a virtual 
option.
